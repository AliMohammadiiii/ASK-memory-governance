\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All results below are recomputed from retained per-item prediction files rather
than transcribed from run reports; the recomputation is in
\texttt{artifacts/analysis/mgp6\_v43\_full\_benchmark\_analysis.json}. We report
\mgpc{} first, because it is the instrument whose construct-validity gate holds,
and then the \mgp{} pilot, whose failed gate is itself a result.

\subsection{\mgpc{} sealed-hidden result}
\label{sec:results:hidden}

One configuration frozen before hidden scoring, the ASK live controller with
public-indexed-evidence routing and the conservative \abstain-only guard, using
\texttt{qwen/qwen3.5-9b}, was selected on public development data and then run
exactly once on the sealed hidden split ($n=540$, coverage $1.000$).
Table~\ref{tab:v43main} reports it. Accuracy is $0.8481$ and macro-F1 is
$0.8479$; the $5{,}000$-replicate component-cluster bootstrap gives
$[0.8037, 0.8889]$ and $[0.8021, 0.8894]$.

Two derived statistics make the balanced design pay off. Cohen's $\kappa$
against gold is $0.7722$ and the multiclass Matthews correlation is $0.7728$;
because the gold split is exactly $180/180/180$, these are not inflated by any
class prior, and the gap between $0.848$ accuracy and $0.77$ agreement is
entirely the structure of the errors rather than their count. Balanced accuracy
equals raw accuracy ($0.8481$) for the same reason. For contrast, the
item-independence Wilson interval is $[0.8154, 0.8760]$, whose width
($0.0605$) is $29\%$ smaller than the cluster width ($0.0852$): reporting only
the Wilson interval would understate the uncertainty by that much, and the
difference is the price of scenario components.

\subsection{Error structure: what the confusion matrix says that accuracy does not}
\label{sec:results:confusion}

Table~\ref{tab:v43confusion} and Figure~\ref{fig:v43-confusion}b give the
hidden-split confusion matrix. The errors are strongly asymmetric, and for a
write-governance system the asymmetry, not the total, is the operational
result.

\supersede{} is the best-recovered action ($0.9167$ recall, $0.9066$ F1):
when the evidence really does establish a new value, the controller applies it.
\abstain{} is the worst ($0.7889$ recall, $0.8208$ F1), and its errors are
lopsided. Of $38$ mishandled gold-\abstain{} items, $30$ became \keep{} and
only $8$ became \supersede{}. That distinction matters operationally. An
\abstain$\to$\keep{} error leaves the stored state byte-identical to what
abstention would have produced. The memory is not corrupted, but the logged
decision records \keep{} rather than the warranted uncertainty refusal. An
\abstain$\to$\supersede{} error actually replaces an active record on
insufficient evidence, which is the failure the action space exists to prevent.
Collapsing both into one ``false commit'' rate of $38/180 = 0.2111$
(CI $[0.1578, 0.2764]$) therefore overstates the harm: the state-corrupting
component is $8/180 = 0.0444$ (CI $[0.0227, 0.0852]$), a factor of five
smaller.

Because the three actions within a component are surface-matched
(Section~\ref{sec:v43}), the confusion profile also names its own mechanism.
\abstain{} is hardest precisely because its indexed evidence asserts
\emph{unresolvability} while four distractor sentences continue to assert
resolvable \keep{} and \supersede{} readings; a system that reads the transcript
instead of the index will be pulled toward a commitment. The development-split
error audit confirms this directly: every mechanically resolvable error is
distractor capture.

The remaining categories are read the same way. Missed updates, meaning gold
\supersede{} not applied, are $15/180 = 0.0833$ (CI $[0.0511, 0.1329]$), so
the system leaves a stale value in place on roughly one in twelve items that
required a change. Destructive overwrites, where a gold-\keep{} record was
superseded, are $11/180 = 0.0611$ (CI $[0.0345, 0.1061]$). Unnecessary
abstention over the $360$ items that did require a decision is
$24/360 = 0.0667$ (CI $[0.0452, 0.0973]$), which is low: the controller is not
buying its safety by refusing to act. Selective behaviour matches: non-abstention
coverage is $0.6926$ with selective accuracy $0.8449$, meaning the items the
controller does commit on are answered slightly \emph{less} accurately than
average, so its abstentions are not well targeted at its own hard cases. This
is a calibration finding, and it is the clearest single defect in the frozen
configuration.

\subsection{Development-split comparison against baselines and ablations}
\label{sec:results:dev}

Because only one hidden run was authorised, every system contrast is on the
public development split ($n=180$, balanced $60/60/60$). We report these as
development evidence and never as confirmatory results: this is the split on
which the prompt, the guard, and the configuration were chosen.
Table~\ref{tab:v43dev} and Figure~\ref{fig:v43-dev} give the full suite,
Table~\ref{tab:v43mcnemar} the paired tests.

The most useful fact in the table is a negative one about the baselines. All
four structural baselines, all four ablations, the zero-shot decision policy,
the five-feature scorecard adapter, and all three adapter-mapped external memory
systems land between $0.2944$ and $0.3667$. This is at or below the $0.3333$
chance rate of a balanced three-way decision. Their macro-F1 values
($0.178$--$0.312$) and Matthews correlations ($-0.069$ to $0.092$) confirm
that this is not near-chance accuracy with useful structure underneath; these
systems are close to uninformative on this task. Two mechanisms produce that
result. The commit-happy systems (B2, B4, the zero-shot policy, and the
no-abstention, no-typing, and no-lineage ablations) supersede almost
everything: their destructive-overwrite rates run $0.767$--$0.983$ and their
false-commit rates $0.900$--$1.000$. The abstain-happy systems (the scorecard
adapter, Mem0, Graphiti) do the opposite: Mem0 abstains on $117/120$ items that
required a decision and misses every one of the $60$ required updates. Both
failure modes score near chance while behaving in opposite ways, which is
precisely why we report the error decomposition rather than accuracy alone.

Against that floor the composed controllers separate cleanly. The frozen
configuration reaches $0.8444$ (CI $[0.789, 0.894]$) and the successor
configuration $0.9500$ as run, $0.9611$ once its guard is composed rather than
replaced. Every controller-versus-baseline and controller-versus-ablation
contrast rejects at Holm-corrected $p < 10^{-17}$, with $b/c$ splits as
one-sided as $98/6$. The successor gain over the frozen configuration is
smaller but still separates ($b=28$, $c=7$, RD $+0.117$, Holm $p = 0.0015$).
The two successor variants do not separate from each other ($b=2$, $c=0$,
Holm $p = 0.5$), so we do not claim the guard-composition fix improved the
successor system, only that it did not damage it while restoring the intended
composition semantics.

\subsection{Which component does the work}
\label{sec:results:guard}

Because the controller composes a rule policy with a model policy, we can score
each component separately on development data (Table~\ref{tab:v43guard},
Figure~\ref{fig:v43-guard}a). Neither alone reaches the composed result. For
the frozen configuration the model action alone scores $0.7500$ and the rule
policy alone also $0.7500$; composing them yields $0.8444$. The guard changed
$27$ of $180$ items, repairing $20$ model errors, breaking $3$ correct model
actions, and leaving $4$ error-neutral. For the successor configuration the
asymmetry is starker: the model alone scores $0.7167$, the guard changes $42$
items, and every one of those changes is a repair. Its rule policy is not
scorable as a standalone action assignment, because that configuration's
policy emits \texttt{model\_preserved} on $120$ of $180$ items by design rather
than proposing an action.

On the hidden split the guard fired $36$ times, always out of \abstain{}
($30 \to \keep$, $6 \to \supersede$). This bounds its contribution: even if
every one of those $36$ overrides were correct and every model \abstain{} it
did not touch were wrong, the guard accounts for at most $6.7$ accuracy points
of the $84.8$ observed. The result is a genuine composition, not a rule engine
with a model attached, nor a model with cosmetic rules.

\subsection{Cost sensitivity}
\label{sec:results:costmatrix}

Accuracy weights every error equally, which no memory-governance deployment
does. Table~\ref{tab:v43cost_matrix} recomputes expected loss under three
pre-specified cost regimes. On the hidden split the frozen controller incurs
$0.1519$ balanced loss, $0.4204$ commit-averse, and $0.2963$ update-responsive.
The commit-averse figure is the largest because that regime charges most
heavily for exactly the controller's dominant error class. Crucially, the
ordering among ASK controllers is stable across all three regimes, unlike the
reference-versus-LLM comparison on the v2 pilot (Section~\ref{sec:v2policy}),
where the preferred system flips with the cost matrix. A deployment that
weights ambiguity-preservation heavily should prefer the successor
configuration on every regime we specified; that conclusion does not depend on
which regime it picks.

\subsection{Computation time and token cost}
\label{sec:results:cost}

Table~\ref{tab:v43cost} and Figure~\ref{fig:v43-cost} give the operational
accounting. All runs used concurrency $1$, temperature $0$, and a $128$-token
generation cap, so the numbers are per-item serial costs and are not a
throughput claim.

The write decision is cheap. On the hidden split, per-item response latency has
mean $1{,}673$~ms, median $1{,}278$~ms, p95 $2{,}931$~ms, and maximum
$12{,}626$~ms, summing to $903.5$~s of model time across $540$ items. The
distribution is right-skewed with a thin provider tail rather than a fat one:
median-to-p95 is a factor of $2.3$, but p95-to-max is a factor of $4.3$, so the
worst-case latency is dominated by a handful of route-level stalls rather than
by hard items. We resist reading these as compute measurements. They are
provider-reported wall times through a shared OpenRouter route; the
deterministic scorecard adapter, which makes no network call, completes the same
$180$ decisions in a $0.13$~ms median.

Token cost separates the systems more informatively than latency does.
Evidence-indexed routing spends $393$ prompt tokens per decision on the hidden
split ($212{,}467$ total) and $7.4$ completion tokens, because the decision is
constrained to emit a single JSON action. The full-context baselines spend
substantially more for a chance-level result: B4 naive overwrite $578$ prompt
tokens per item, B2 flat retrieval $597$, and the zero-shot policy $549$. The
successor configuration costs more than the frozen one ($506$ versus $396$
prompt tokens per item on development) and buys $10.6$ accuracy points for that $28\%$
increase. The only cheaper live system, B3 recency at $233$ tokens, achieves
$0.3278$ accuracy.

Monetary cost is reported as \emph{unavailable}, not estimated. OpenRouter
response telemetry carried no price or cost field on any of these runs. Given
the frozen model identifier, a list-price multiplication would be arithmetically
easy and epistemically empty. Routing, provider selection, and caching all
affect the billed amount, and none of that is in our logs. We record the token
counts, which are measured, and decline to convert them into a currency figure
that is not.

Run integrity on the hidden split was clean: $0$ in-client retries, $0$ invalid
or unparseable responses, $0$ duplicate predictions, and one transport-level
disconnect after $432$ predictions that the immutable-manifest resume recovered
under identical controls. One caveat on the manifest: its
$207.96$~s \texttt{wall\_clock\_seconds} field records only the resumed
invocation that produced the final $108$ predictions, so it should not be read
as the end-to-end duration of the $540$-item run. The summed model latency of
$903.5$~s is the defensible aggregate.

\subsection{The v2 shortcut check fails, and why v4.3 exists}
\label{sec:v2shortcut}

The remainder of this section reports the historical \mgp{} pilot. Its results
are retained because they motivate the v4.3 redesign, and because the shortcut
failure they document is the paper's most transferable finding about diagnostic
construction. None of them supports a capability claim.

The conflict-type-only lookup fitted on train+dev reaches $1.0000$ accuracy and
macro-F1 on test; the coarser six-way type lookup reaches $0.9667$ accuracy and
$0.9471$ macro-F1. Neither sees the dialogue or memory state. Thus the held-out
item split does not test whether a system reasons about evidence quality,
provenance, or temporal scope: scenario recognition is sufficient. This failed
check dominates every policy comparison below.

\subsection{Safety--responsiveness trade-off on the v2 pilot}

The reference policy makes no action false commits on the 33 test abstention items, but
the exact one-sided 95\% upper bound is $0.0868$, not zero risk. It misses
$10/45$ required updates ($0.2222$) and unnecessarily abstains on $10/57$
non-abstention items ($0.1754$). The structured LLM action false-commit rate is
$2/33=0.0606$, while its missed-update rate is $7/45=0.1556$. Consequently the
reference policy has lower expected loss under the commit-averse matrix
($0.111$ vs.\ $0.189$), but higher loss under the update-responsive matrix
($0.556$ vs.\ $0.433$). Safety is therefore cost-conditional, not an absolute
ordering. Top-label ECE is $0.156$ for the reference confidence and $0.302$ for
the LLM confidence; both are descriptive because neither confidence was
calibrated on an independent set.

\subsection{State and component diagnostics on the v2 pilot}

Full-corpus ablations are retained only as within-generator mechanism checks.
Removing abstention raises invalid-commit from $0.0$ to $1.0$; removing
provenance yields $0.606$; removing typing drives \keep{} F1 to $0.0$. Removing
lineage leaves action accuracy unchanged at $0.9222$ but drops lineage integrity
$0.8556\to0.3689$. The last result demonstrates the central measurement point:
action accuracy alone cannot reveal a corrupted transition history.

\subsection{What transfers from v2 to v4.3}

The v2 shortcut failure is not repaired by the v4.3 numbers; it is
\emph{avoided} by a different construction. Three v2 findings do transfer.
First, a lookup probe over metadata is a cheap and decisive construct-validity
gate, and the v4.3 build runs one as a release gate rather than as a
post-hoc audit. Second, the lineage ablation, with action accuracy unchanged at
$0.9222$ while lineage integrity falls $0.8556 \to 0.3689$, shows that a
decision-level score cannot detect a corrupted transition history, which is why
the v4.3 evaluation contract keeps state and action scoring separate. Third,
the cost-matrix flip in the v2 reference-versus-LLM comparison established that
``safer'' is not a system property but a property of a system under a stated
cost regime; Section~\ref{sec:results:costmatrix} therefore reports v4.3 under
three regimes rather than one.
