\section{Downstream and Runtime Evaluation: \lme{}}
\label{sec:downstream}

To test whether write-time governance helps a long-horizon task, we evaluate a
stratified $180$-item slice of \lme{} ($6$ competencies $\times\,30$, seed $0$).
We report the complete-coverage full-slice final candidate (freeze
\texttt{final-2.0}, denoted V12), then document why the earlier constructions
on this track were ineligible and are retained only as history. Throughout we
use the exact ledger labels \texttt{diagnostic\_only},
\texttt{failure\_replay\_only}, \texttt{incomplete}, and
\texttt{final\_candidate}, and do not collapse them
into a generic result.

\subsection{The V12 full-slice final candidate}
\label{sec:downstream:final}

The V12 run evaluates all $180$ items with a single answer model
(\texttt{qwen/qwen3.5-9b}) and a single judge (\texttt{google/gemma-4-31b-it}),
with logged judge routing and \emph{zero} judge-model fallbacks. The five system
rows share the evaluated protocol, but the run-level homogeneity audit remains
false because one launcher hash is stale. On this run the
\ask{} controller reaches $137/180 = 76.1\%$ accuracy (exact $95\%$ CI
$70.0$--$82.2$), the highest of the five evaluated memory systems and $12.8$
points above the strongest structural baseline (naive overwrite,
$114/180 = 63.3\%$). Answerable-only accuracy is $75.4\%$ over $171$ answerable
items and abstention accuracy is $88.9\%$ over $9$ abstention items. The
ordering is monotone in memory sophistication: no memory ($5.0\%$) $<$ recency
($23.9\%$) $<$ flat-vector retrieval ($44.4\%$) $<$ naive overwrite ($63.3\%$)
$<$ governed writes ($76.1\%$).

Monotone orderings invite over-reading, so we tested each adjacent step
(Table~\ref{tab:lme_v12}). Every step separates under an exact paired McNemar
test, Holm-corrected over the four predeclared adjacent tests: recency over no memory
($p = 4.7\times10^{-10}$), flat vector over recency ($p = 3.7\times10^{-6}$),
naive overwrite over flat vector ($p = 6.8\times10^{-6}$), and governed writes
over naive overwrite ($b=31$, $c=8$, RD $+0.128$, $p = 2.9\times10^{-4}$). The
last of these is the paper's downstream claim, and it is a within-slice
comparison under one frozen protocol, not a general capability estimate.

Per competency the gain over naive overwrite is not uniform, and that
non-uniformity is the strongest available evidence that the downstream
improvement comes from the write decision rather than from generic pipeline
differences: a better retriever would not preferentially help knowledge-update
items. Section~\ref{sec:downstream:competency} reports the full five-system
breakdown and the per-competency significance treatment.

\subsection{Write-decision behaviour and downstream cost}

The controller's action log records $12{,}654$ write decisions across $3{,}547$
sessions: $11{,}478$ \keep{} ($90.7\%$), $779$ \abstain{} ($6.2\%$), and $397$
\supersede{} ($3.1\%$). The distribution is what the design predicts on this
multi-session benchmark dialogue, where most turns carry no conflicting
evidence, and it is very different from the balanced \mgpc{} diagnostic. This is
the reason we keep both instruments. Every one of the $397$ supersessions recorded both a
predecessor and a successor identifier, and none of the $779$ abstentions
produced a resulting record. These identifier-level checks support retained
lineage and no-commit abstention on the logged path. They are not a replay of
the generic ASK invariant validator.

\subsection{Five-dimensional scorecard: type comparison and action mechanism}
\label{sec:downstream:scorecard}

The action log is sufficiently complete to reproduce the live policy without a
model call: each row carries $(c,e,f,r,\rho)$, both scores, both
type-conditional thresholds, and the action. Replaying
Equation~\ref{eq:scorecard} matches both stored scores and the final action on
$12{,}654/12{,}654$ rows exactly. The framework in
Section~\ref{sec:scorecard} is therefore not an architectural proposal or
post-hoc explanation; it is the decision rule that generated every V12 write.

Table~\ref{tab:lme-scorecard-types} compares actions by memory type. Episodic
facts dominate the extracted population ($9{,}857$, $77.9\%$) and account for
$364/397$ supersessions. Stable preferences are rare ($381$, $3.0\%$), have the
highest abstention rate ($13.4\%$), and the lowest supersession rate ($1.0\%$).
Transient preferences lie between them in volume but not action mix: $9.8\%$
abstain and only $1.2\%$ supersede. These differences are descriptive rather
than a type effect, because the feature distributions also differ. The
median marker burden is $0$ for episodic and stable records but $0.5$ for
transient records.

\input{tables/lme_scorecard}

The counterfactual replay in Table~\ref{tab:lme-scorecard-ablation} identifies
which dimensions actually determine actions on this support. Omitting $f$
removes every observed abstention: $721$ become \keep{} and $58$ become
\supersede{}. Omitting $r$ removes all $397$ supersessions and converts another
$86$ abstentions to \keep{}. By contrast, omitting evidence quality $e$ or the
stability prior $\rho$ changes no action, and omitting confidence $c$ changes
only eight. This does not make $e$, $\rho$, or $c$ useless in principle: they
move score margins and can act on inputs near a boundary. It does show that the
observed V12 action partition is carried almost entirely by marker burden and
signed relation.

The most consequential result is about the rule's discrete guard. Of the
$11{,}478$ \keep{} decisions, $11{,}091$ ($96.6\%$) already satisfy
$S_{\mathrm{sup}}>\theta_{\mathrm{sup}}$. They remain \keep{} only because
$r$ supplies no update pressure. Removing that guard changes all $11{,}091$ to
\supersede{} ($87.6\%$ of the entire log). Thus the weighted supersession score
is not independently selective on this run; the signed-relation gate is the
safety-critical component.

Type enters through $\rho$ and the thresholds, but is nearly inert here. Setting
all $\rho$ values to the episodic prior changes zero decisions; replacing all
type-specific thresholds with the episodic thresholds changes only five
stable-preference \abstain{} decisions to \keep{} ($0.04\%$ of the log).
The type ordering is a transparent policy commitment, while the observed
between-type action rates mostly reflect candidate and relation features. Since
\lme{} supplies no gold write action per extracted candidate, this analysis
establishes execution and decision dependence, not gold action correctness.
The downstream QA comparison remains the separate outcome.

\subsection{Where the compute actually goes}
\label{sec:downstream:pipeline}

The retained pipeline traces let us decompose the controller's cost by stage
rather than reporting a single per-item figure, and the decomposition overturns
the natural reading of the accuracy table (Table~\ref{tab:lme_pipeline},
Figure~\ref{fig:lme-pipeline}a).

Answering the $180$ questions takes $180$ model calls and $4.07$M prompt
tokens. Getting to the point where those questions \emph{can} be answered takes
$31{,}004$ further calls and $52.7$M prompt tokens: $11{,}847$ extraction calls
over $11{,}491$ dialogue turns ($41.8$M tokens, $73.6\%$ of the budget) and
$19{,}157$ relation-classification calls over $9{,}539$ candidate--prior pairs
($10.9$M tokens, $19.2\%$). The write-time stages are $92.8\%$ of prompt tokens
and $99.4\%$ of model calls. The answer stage, which is the only stage an
end-to-end memory benchmark scores, is $7.2\%$ of the token budget.

This reframes the cost comparison in Table~\ref{tab:lme_v12}. The governed
controller is not an expensive answerer; it is a cheap answerer sitting on an
expensive ingestion pipeline, and the same is true of naive overwrite. Reporting
only answer-stage tokens, as the retrieval-only baselines invite, measures the
$7\%$ and ignores the $93\%$.

\paragraph{A correction to how per-item latency should be read.}
The \texttt{latency\_ms} field on each answer record is \emph{cumulative
end-to-end} time for that item, not answer-generation time. Summing it gives
$40.46$~h, while the summed extraction and relation model latency is $38.91$~h;
the $1.55$~h difference is the implied generation-only cost, about $31$~s per
item rather than the $809$~s the field suggests. Per-item pipeline times quoted
in Table~\ref{tab:lme_v12} and elsewhere use the cumulative field and should be
read as end-to-end pipeline time. We flag this explicitly because the naive
reading, that a single answer call takes thirteen minutes, is wrong by more
than an order of magnitude.

\paragraph{Extraction is mostly rejection.}
Of the $11{,}491$ processed turns, $5{,}656$ yield
\texttt{no\_extractable\_content}, which is $49.2\%$. The extractor's most
common output is
\emph{nothing}, which is the correct behaviour on conversational filler and the
first line of defence against memory bloat. The remaining turns produce
$11{,}771$ candidate memories ($1.02$ per turn overall), typed as $9{,}144$
episodic facts, $2{,}261$ transient preferences, and $366$ stable preferences.
Mean extraction confidence is $0.934$ (minimum $0.75$), $17.4\%$ of candidates
carry a hedge marker, and $1.3\%$ carry a negation marker. Parsing was robust:
$370$ of $11{,}491$ turns hit a first-parse error, $9$ required the repair path,
and there were zero model fallbacks and zero retries beyond the first attempt.

\paragraph{Relation routing is deliberately conservative.}
The relation stage is two-stage: stage one labels the candidate against a
retrieved prior as \textsf{support}, \textsf{contradict}, or
\textsf{independent}; stage two, invoked on $9{,}530$ of $9{,}539$
classifications, resolves same-subject and temporal ordering. Routing is then
deterministic, and the released trace records the reason for every decision
(Figure~\ref{fig:lme-pipeline}c). All $8{,}281$ \textsf{independent} labels route
to \textsf{unclear}; an independent candidate is never allowed to displace a
prior. Of $369$ \textsf{contradict} labels, only the $221$ that stage two
confirms as \emph{same-subject and newer} route to \textsf{supersede}; $113$
older and $33$ same-time contradictions are held as \textsf{contradict} without
a write. So $221$ of $9{,}539$ classifications ($2.3\%$) are permitted to
propose a replacement, which is the mechanism behind the $397$ supersessions
($3.1\%$) in the action log and, ultimately, behind the controller's low
destructive-overwrite rate.

\paragraph{Caching did not help, and the trace says why.}
The extraction cache holds $11{,}477$ unique keys against $11{,}491$ traced
turns and the relation cache $9{,}523$ against $9{,}539$ classifications,
under $0.3\%$ deduplication in both. Each turn is keyed by its own content and
each candidate by its pairing with a specific prior, so in a single pass over
distinct sessions there is essentially nothing to reuse. The caches are
insurance for resumes, not a throughput optimisation, and any deployment
estimate that assumes cache amortisation across a first pass is unsupported by
this evidence.

\subsection{The complete system comparison}
\label{sec:downstream:full}

Table~\ref{tab:lme_full} and Figure~\ref{fig:lme-full} give the full
five-system comparison on one homogeneous slice: accuracy with intervals, an
error decomposition, per-item latency, and separated answer and ingestion token
costs. Three things in it are not visible from an accuracy ranking.

\paragraph{Accuracy alone conflates two very different failures.}
Decomposing the $180$ items separates a wrong answer from a declined one
(Figure~\ref{fig:lme-full}b). B1 with no memory is not merely inaccurate; it
refuses $170$ of $171$ answerable items, which is the correct behaviour for a
system with nothing to retrieve, and its $5\%$ comes almost entirely from
correctly declining the $9$ unanswerable items. B3 recency refuses $115$. Read as
accuracy these look like the same kind of failure as a wrong answer; they are
not. The governed controller and naive overwrite are the only two systems that
actually commit to answers at scale, and between them the interesting number is
the wrong-answer count: $25$ against $49$. The controller roughly halves wrong
answers ($-49\%$) while adding two over-refusals, and a paired exact McNemar
test restricted to wrong answers gives $b=31$, $c=7$,
$p = 1.2\times10^{-4}$.

\paragraph{Only the controller both commits and stays calibrated.}
On the $9$ by-design unanswerable items, naive overwrite answers $2$ and the
controller answers $1$. The absolute counts are too small to separate,
we report them because a system that commits more often should be checked for
exactly this failure, not because $2$ versus $1$ is a result.

\paragraph{The expensive part is ingestion, not governance.}
This is the finding that most changes the cost story
(Figure~\ref{fig:lme-full}c). B3 recency, a baseline whose whole premise is
that it is cheap, runs an extraction stage of $43.0$M prompt tokens, which is
$3\%$ \emph{more} than the governed controller's $41.8$M, and scores $23.9\%$
against $76.1\%$. Extraction is the dominant cost and it is common to every
write-time system. The governance machinery proper, relation classification,
is $10.9$M tokens, a $26\%$ marginal increment on extraction, and that
increment is what separates $23.9\%$ from $76.1\%$. The controller does have the
largest total prompt cost ($56.8$M against B3's $43.2$M) because it is the only
system that runs both stages; the point is not that governance is free but that
it is a small fraction of a cost every memory-maintaining system already pays.
Framing governed writes as \emph{the} expensive option misplaces the accounting:
what is expensive is deciding what a turn contains.

\subsection{Where the gain lands, by competency}
\label{sec:downstream:competency}

Figure~\ref{fig:lme-competency} and Table~\ref{tab:lme_competency_full} break
all five systems down by competency. The controller leads on every one of the
six, but the $+23$-item margin over naive overwrite is not spread evenly: $+8$
on knowledge update, $+5$ on single-session preference, $+3$ each on
single-session assistant and temporal reasoning, and $+2$ each on multi-session
and single-session user.

That concentration is the mechanistic evidence. Knowledge update is precisely
the competency that requires recognising a superseding fact and replacing the
prior value, which is the decision the write policy governs; it is where a
governed write should help most, and it is where it helps most ($29/30$ against
$21/30$). Single-session preference is the second-largest gain and also the
competency where the controller's abstention is most active ($6$ over-refusals),
consistent with the v7 error taxonomy in Section~\ref{sec:v7taxonomy}
attributing those failures to retrieval and answer realisation rather than to
the write policy.

Under Holm-corrected paired exact McNemar tests across the six competencies,
only knowledge update separates individually ($p = 0.047$); the other five have
corrected $p = 1.0$. At $n = 30$ per competency this is expected, and we state
the implication plainly: the per-competency pattern is \emph{descriptive
evidence about where the effect concentrates}, while the reliable inferential
claim is the aggregate contrast ($p = 2.9\times10^{-4}$). A reader should not
take any single competency cell as independently established.

\subsection{Downstream cost in context}

Per-item end-to-end pipeline time is $809$~s for the governed controller and
$966$~s for naive overwrite, against $1.6$--$3.1$~s for the three retrieval-only
baselines, a factor of roughly $320$ against flat-vector retrieval, buying
$31.7$ accuracy points. Answer-stage prompt tokens show the same ordering
($22{,}625$ per item versus $14{,}227$ and $3{,}629$), but as
Section~\ref{sec:downstream:pipeline} shows, that comparison understates the
true gap by more than an order of magnitude, because the retrieval-only
baselines have no ingestion pipeline to account for. Whether the exchange is
worthwhile is a deployment question we do not adjudicate; we report the full
accounting so it is not hidden behind the accuracy ordering.

\paragraph{The deterministic computation aid is auditable and net neutral.}
The governed and naive-overwrite pipelines also run a deterministic
date/numeric/list solver over retrieved evidence. It recognized an intent on
$28$ controller items and $39$ naive-overwrite items, but no item bypassed the
answer model. Controller-side verification replaced the model's final answer
twice: one date-difference replacement repaired an error and one ordinal-list
replacement introduced an error, for zero net change in accuracy. Thus the
$23$-item aggregate advantage over naive overwrite cannot be attributed to
deterministic answer substitution. The preserved traces make this helper a
separately testable answer-layer component rather than an unreported part of
the write-policy effect.

Every item was scored by the single frozen judge: $40$ items were resolved by
deterministic guards (normalised match, no LLM call), $140$ went to the LLM
judge, and $2$ scoring fallbacks were logged. Nine items received a documented
semantic-equivalence adjudication that corrected judge scoring errors (for
example, accepting ``7 days'' as equivalent to ``1 week''); each carries a
written rationale and a preserved pre-adjudication backup of the machine
judgment.

Two provenance-bookkeeping items remain open on this run and do not affect any
reported number: the run's homogeneity gate flags a stale code/protocol hash for
the evaluation launcher, and the manifest was not written to a clean terminal
status after a resume. The per-system integrity checks (answer model, judge
routing, fallback counts) pass with empty error and warning lists.

\subsection{The contaminated v7 diagnostic}

The earlier v7 controller run is provider-contaminated: it accumulated $316$
HTTP-402 credit-exhaustion traces, $388$ empty-extraction fallbacks, at least
$12$ manual relaunches, and a no-op escalation tier (the ``big'' and ``fast''
models were identical). It is therefore \texttt{diagnostic\_only}, and on it
naive overwrite ($114/180$) edged the controller ($110/180$). The homogeneous
V12 run above supersedes that diagnostic and reverses the ordering under
controlled conditions.

\subsection{Why the 77.2\% construction was ineligible}

The v8 replay selected exactly the $70$ v7 controller failures and repaired
$25/70$. It was not a rerun of the full slice. The reported $139/180=77.2\%$
then combined $110$ carried-forward v7 successes ($108$ original
Gemma-judged successes plus two manual v7 adjudications), $25$ Qwen replay
repairs, and four later Codex/GPT-5 overrides. Before the four overrides, the
same construction was $135/180=75.0\%$. Judge identity and pipeline version are
therefore confounded with whether an item had previously failed, and the fixes
were evaluated on the failures that motivated them. The ledger correctly labels
both figures \texttt{failure\_replay\_only}, not final or diagnostic full-run
accuracy.

The top-up is non-empty and directly contradicts the carry-forward assumption.
It re-executed $84/110$ former v7 successes under the v8 attempt: $71$ remained
correct and $13$ regressed. Combining those $71$ with the $25$ raw replay
repairs gives $96/154$ observed. Treating the $26$ unresolved items as all wrong
or all correct gives full-slice raw bounds of $53.3$--$67.8\%$. Retaining the
four replay overrides gives bounds of $55.6$--$70.0\%$. Even the optimistic
bound is below $77.2\%$, so this attempt cannot reproduce the carry-forward
construction. These are missing-data bounds, not confidence intervals.

\subsection{Single-session preference: carry-forward artifact and taxonomy}
\label{sec:v7taxonomy}

Single-session preference (SSP) was the weakest v7 competency at $11/30$. That
is a contaminated diagnostic run only, not a clean v8 measurement.
The replay targeted the $19$ v7 SSP failures and repaired $0/19$; the top-up
completed $10/11$ former SSP successes and retained only $8/10$. Across the two
selected subsets, $8/29$ completed SSP items are correct and one is unresolved,
so the maximum is $9/30$. The earlier statement that SSP stayed ``unchanged at
$11/30$'' is only a carry-forward artifact.

The replay and v7 prompt contexts differ, so persistence of the $19$ failures
does \emph{not} isolate the answer model or support a same-context causal claim.
It does, however, identify a concrete set for descriptive error analysis. By
inspecting whether each decisive gold fact appeared in the constructed prompt,
we classified the $19$ v7 failures as shown in Table~\ref{tab:ssp}.
Grounding-supply failures (retrieval omission plus cross-session contamination)
are primary in $10/19$ and present in $15/19$; answer-side failures are primary
in $9/19$. This taxonomy describes the inspected artifacts and does not prove
that the write policy is causally exonerated.

\subsection{Proposed intervention and remaining headroom}

The taxonomy motivates a proposed but unexecuted intervention: represent each
preference as a typed record (holder/target/attribute/polarity/scope/stability/
source), require retrieval to surface the supporting-session record for the
query subject, and constrain answers to retrieved, grounded preferences while
honoring explicit negatives. We make no accuracy claim for this design. It is
directed at the competency where the V12 controller has the most headroom:
single-session preference remains its weakest competency at $53.3\%$, alongside
multi-session and temporal reasoning at $63.3\%$ each, whereas knowledge update
and single-session assistant are effectively solved at $96.7\%$
(Table~\ref{tab:lme_competency_full}).



The V12 full-slice final candidate described in
Section~\ref{sec:downstream:final} is the eligible downstream result. It
evaluates all $180$ items with one answer model and logged judge routing under
the frozen final protocol. The experiment registry moves this run from
\texttt{externally\_pending} to \texttt{final\_candidate} with the controller at
$76.1\%$; promotion to \texttt{final} in the canonical results ledger is pending
the two open provenance-bookkeeping items (stale launcher hash, non-finalized
manifest status), which are tracked separately and do not enter the reported
score.
